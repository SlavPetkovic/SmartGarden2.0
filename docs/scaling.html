Bus, Mux or Node

One houseplant indoors and a bed of ten outdoors are not the same system twice. They are two zones, two buses and two environment stations — and the outdoor one cannot be built the way the indoor one is.

A node answers distance. A multiplexer answers everything that comes after the fourth plant — and out in the bed, that includes the wiring itself.

What ten plants cost in calibration

One soak, ten curves — not ten soaks.

Thresholds describe a probe at a depth in a soil mix, and ten plants in one bed share all three. Run the bed once with every probe logging at the same time and you get ten drying curves in parallel; they will cluster. Set the zone default from the cluster and override only the plants that genuinely differ — which is exactly what DATA-5 means by a plant inheriting zone defaults where its own are unset. Ten plants is one soak, not ten.

Sequencing

Decided: finish Phase 1 indoors first.

Every layer gets proven on a machine you can reach and reset before anything goes in the ground. The known cost is seasonal: outdoor soil in September behaves differently from outdoor soil in June, and Phase 2 wants several weeks across varied conditions, so the calendar date the node arrives on decides which season the first outdoor record describes. Nothing stops the bed being dug, wired and enclosed in parallel — that work has no software dependency.

Nothing here changes Phase 1. The multiplexer is already in the layer 03 gate (SENS-4, “since Seesaw offers only four addresses and the target is dozens of plants”); the node entity is layer 02 and the ingest endpoint is layer 05. See docs/BUILD-PLAN.md §7a.
